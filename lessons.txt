================================================================================
LESSONS — what this project actually taught, beyond the results
Compiled 2026-08-20. Written for whoever runs the next eval, including me.
================================================================================

THE ONE THAT MATTERS MOST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A benchmark harness reports a number whether or not it measured anything.
Nothing in Inspect, llama.cpp, or inspect_evals warned that Qwen3.8 had answered
zero of 33 cloning questions. It returned 0.121 with a straight face. Every
other lesson below is a variation on this.

Corollary: a sub-chance score on multiple choice is a plumbing bug, not a
capability finding. Below the guessing floor means answers are not being
produced. Treat it as a crash, not a result.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. DEFAULTS YOU DID NOT CHOOSE ARE STILL CHOICES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
* The model's chat template injected `reasoning_effort: xhigh` ("think carefully,
  validate key assumptions, consider plausible alternatives"). We never set it.
  It was excellent for AIME (0.967) and fatal for long-input MCQ. Read the chat
  template your server actually loads -- llama-server prints the rendered example
  at startup, which is the cheapest place to catch this.
* Sampling was unset at every level, so llama.cpp defaults applied. In llama.cpp
  EVERY anti-repetition sampler ships disabled: repeat_penalty 1.00, presence
  0.00, frequency 0.00, dry_multiplier 0.00. On repetitive low-entropy input
  (DNA), the model fell into degenerate loops and burned its whole budget.
* "Leave it at defaults for comparability" was argued here and was wrong. If the
  default is broken, matching it propagates the break. Record the config
  difference instead of inheriting the bug.

2. FIXES OVERSHOOT; MEASURE THE FIX TOO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DRY sampling at allowed-length 8 eliminated the loops AND suppressed legitimate
repetitive work (copying plasmid DNA), dropping accuracy 0.200 -> 0.133 with one
sample stopping after 450 characters. At allowed-length 32 accuracy went to
0.467. The first fix looked successful on the metric it targeted (loops: zero)
while making the actual result worse. Always check the fix against the outcome
you care about, not the symptom you were chasing.

3. ANY CAP BECOMES A CONFOUND WHEN IT CORRELATES WITH WHAT YOU MEASURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the most transferable finding, and it is subtle because the numbers stay
plausible.
* XSTest generated at max_tokens=256. Completions ending mid-sentence: 40% /
  27% / 45% across arms -- but concentrated in the SAFE subset (64% / 37% / 65%)
  versus the unsafe subset (10% / 15% / 21%). Why? Refusals are short and
  survive the cap; compliant answers are long and get cut. The cap therefore
  hits one side of the classification, and hits the two compared models
  differently. The headline over-refusal comparison is drawn from exactly that
  subset and those arms.
* StrongREJECT (cap 2048): 3 abliterated samples ran to exactly 2048 and were
  graded "refusal". A 2048-token output is almost certainly not a refusal --
  they are near-certainly truncated compliances misgraded. ~1 point, and in the
  conservative direction (it understates abliteration's effect).
* Precedent in the same project: the JUDGE was truncated at 512 tokens before
  emitting its verdict, more often on the normal model's longer hedged answers.
  Re-grading at 1024 moved abliterated unsafe-refusal 0.934 -> 0.788.
Same bug at three layers: generation cap, judge cap, context ceiling.
Rule: report cap-hit rate PER CONDITION. Asymmetry is the signal, not the mean.

4. VERIFY COMPLETENESS; NEVER TRUST A GLOB
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A killed server leaves a partial .eval that still matches *.eval and looks fine.
Two different partials appeared here: one at 280/313 and one at 0/313. A range
test (0 < n < expected) catches the first and misses the second. The load-bearing
check is status == "success" AND exact expected n. Assert it before grading, not
after. Quarantine partials by renaming so they cannot match.

5. GRADE CACHES ARE SHARED STATE WITH NO OWNER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Per-item grades were cached by key in one directory. Code that globbed the whole
directory merged grades from other models and reported 19 forbidden-request
compliances for a model with a measured refusal rate of 1.000. The contradiction
is what exposed it -- there was no error. Key cache reads to a specific log; never
aggregate across a cache directory. A helper elsewhere that deleted low-parse
cache files by glob would have silently destroyed another run's grades.

6. CROSS-CHECKS THAT EARNED THEIR COST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
* Second judge on identical logs: max disagreement 1.3 points. Converts "how
  much of this is your grader?" from an objection into a measured quantity.
* Placebo condition: a neutral, non-safety wrapper raised refusal 82.8% -> 88.5%.
  Without it, "safety instructions work" is indistinguishable from "instructions
  work". It is the cheapest arm and the one that makes the others interpretable.
* Per-item divergence: aggregates hid the mechanism. Abliteration moved 83
  prompts one way and 1 the other (a single lowered threshold); a prompt attack
  moved 32 one way and 19 the other (perturbation, p=0.09). Same kind of headline
  gap, completely different mechanism, visible only per item.
* Check what the judge is READING. One jailbreak mandates a decoy refusal before
  the real answer; grading the raw completion scores the preamble, not the reply.

7. PROCESS HAZARDS (SHARED MACHINE, LONG UNATTENDED RUNS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Three distinct mechanisms, all hit in one session:
* Edits do not reach a RUNNING bash script -- it holds its parsed copy. Restart
  at a safe boundary. Resume markers make that free.
* Truncate-and-rewrite CORRUPTS a script bash is still reading incrementally.
  Use rename-based writes (sed -i, os.replace); verify the inode changed.
* A trap does NOT fire while a foreground child is running. SIGTERM to an
  orchestrator mid-`inspect eval` does nothing until the child returns. Kill the
  process tree if you need to stop now.
Also: never kill a server you did not start. A bare
`pgrep -x llama-server | xargs kill` destroyed another session's run. Record the
PID you launched and kill only that; wait for VRAM held by others, never reclaim
it. And tell collaborators when you restart -- a gate keyed to your old PID fails
silently and invisibly.

8. TOOL-SPECIFIC TRAPS WORTH REMEMBERING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
* `inspect eval --timeout` does NOT set the HTTP client timeout. The OpenAI SDK
  default (600s) silently caps long generations. Pass -M client_timeout=N.
* llama-server divides context across parallel slots: --parallel 4 with -c 32768
  gives each request 8192.
* Passing -ngl explicitly DISABLES llama.cpp's auto-fit. `llama-fit-params`
  measures the real ceiling instead of predicting it.
* The naive KV-cache formula is wrong for hybrid attention/SSM models. It
  predicted 8.7 GB at 65k context against ~2.1 GB measured, because only ~16 of
  65 layers cache. Trust a load test.
* `hf download` raises a traceback ending in `click.exceptions.Exit: 0` on
  SUCCESS, which the wrapper turns into process exit 1. Scripts branching on its
  status will read failure on success.
* GGUF arch strings differ from HF model_type ("qwen35" vs "qwen3_5"). Grepping
  the wrong spelling will tell you a supported model is unsupported.

9. PUBLISHING AND PROVENANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
* Removing something in a new commit does NOT remove it from a public repo.
  Force-pushing a rewrite leaves the old commits fetchable by SHA. Only deleting
  and recreating the repository removes them. Verified by fetching the old SHA
  before and after.
* Commit metadata is content: author email is public on every commit of a public
  repo. GitHub also records the PUSHER in its public events feed, so pushing from
  the wrong account links identities regardless of commit authorship.
* Decide what is publishable before the first commit, not after. Two history
  rewrites were needed here -- one for identity, one for jailbreak text -- and the
  second was only necessary because the first question was asked late.
* Keep harmful prompt text out of the repo but reproducible locally: cite the
  paper, withhold the text, gitignore the working copy.

10. HONEST REPORTING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
* State what a number licenses. "At a matched 65,536 context this model fails
  the task" is supportable; "this model is worse at molecular cloning" is not,
  when it never answered.
* A pilot is not a benchmark. 0.467 on 15 samples does not become a reportable
  score by being encouraging.
* Do not tune until the number looks plausible and then report it. Fix causes you
  can name, record every parameter changed, and say which comparisons are no
  longer like-for-like.
* Contradictions are the cheapest bug detector available. Every silent failure in
  this project was caught because two numbers disagreed -- refusal rate 1.000
  against 19 compliances, a sub-chance MCQ score, a 2048-token "refusal".
